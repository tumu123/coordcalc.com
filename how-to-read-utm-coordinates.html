How to Read UTM Coordinates (Zone, Band, Easting, Northing)

A UTM coordinate is four facts written as one string. Read any part of it out of order, or drop one of them, and the coordinate stops describing a place — it becomes a set of numbers that could belong anywhere on Earth. This guide takes a single UTM reference apart, explains what each piece means, and covers the misreadings that cause the most trouble in the field and in GIS.

GuideUpdated 9 October 2026Datum: WGS84 / GRS80

What UTM actually is

Universal Transverse Mercator divides the world between 80° S and 84° N into 60 vertical zones, each 6° of longitude wide. Every zone is projected onto its own plane using a transverse cylinder, so within a zone you work in metres on a flat grid rather than in degrees. Zone 1 spans 180° W to 174° W and the numbering runs east, so zone 31 covers 0° to 6° E and zone 60 ends back at 180°, with the 60° to 180° range handled by the polar systems rather than UTM.

Overlaid on the zones is a second division into latitude bands, each 8° of latitude tall, lettered from C at 80° S up to X at 84° N, skipping I and O so they cannot be confused with 1 and 0. That gives 20 bands, and together the zone number and band letter name an area roughly 6° by 8° — about 670 km wide near the equator and always 8° tall.

Read together, that is 43.642562° N, 79.387143° W in Toronto — a point the zone and band already told us was in eastern Canada, since zone 17 with band T can only be the Great Lakes region.

Why the easting starts at 500,000

The easting on a UTM grid is not measured from the zone edge, it is measured from the zone's central meridian, with a false easting of 500,000 m added so that no coordinate comes out negative. An easting of exactly 500,000 therefore sits precisely on the central meridian, an easting smaller than 500,000 is west of it and a larger one is east of it. The default extent of a zone is about ±334 km either side, so a normal easting stays between roughly 166,000 and 834,000 — though each zone is extended 100 km into its neighbours to give a working overlap, which is why eastings between 100,000 and 200,000 or between 800,000 and 900,000 are also valid.

A zone-17 coordinate with an easting of 500,000 and a northing of 4,500,000 resolves to 40.650857° N, 81.000000° W — exactly on the central meridian, as the arithmetic promises. That is a useful sanity check whenever you suspect an easting has been corrupted.

Northing, and the southern hemisphere

In the northern hemisphere the northing is simply the distance north of the equator, from 0 at the equator to about 9,300,000 m at 84° N. In the southern hemisphere the same grid would give negative values, so a false northing of 10,000,000 m is applied: a southern northing of 6,250,948 is 10,000,000 minus 6,250,948, which is 3,749,052 m south of the equator — 33.8688° S, 151.2093° E, which is Sydney.

This is why the band letter is not decoration. Northing alone cannot tell you which hemisphere you are in, because two points 3,749 km north and south of the equator share the same northing magnitude. The band letter settles it, and so does the zone's hemisphere convention.

Order matters: easting before northing

UTM coordinates are written easting first, northing second — X before Y, exactly like a national grid reference. The mnemonic that sticks is along the corridor, then up the stairs: travel along the easting axis before climbing the northing axis. Many countries and most map series follow this convention, but latitude and longitude are usually written the other way round — latitude first — which is the single most common source of confusion when a list of numbers arrives without labels.

Four ways to misread a UTM coordinate. Swapping easting and northing — the numbers usually differ by an order of magnitude, but not always. Dropping the zone — the same easting and northing exists in all 60 zones, so the point could be on any continent. Assuming a band letter is a zone — T is a band, 17 is a zone, and they are never interchangeable. And reading a UTM coordinate as if it were latitude and longitude, which produces a number that looks plausible and is meaningless.

How many digits, how much precision

The number of digits in an easting or northing tells you the precision it was recorded at, because UTM is metric. Whole metres need six digits of northing and six of easting; a tenth of a metre needs seven; millimetres need eight. A coordinate truncated to five digits is only good to 10 metres, and it should not be compared with one recorded to the millimetre without a note that the two are not the same quality of measurement.
