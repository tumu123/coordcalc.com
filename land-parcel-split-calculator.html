Land Parcel Split Calculator

This land parcel split calculator divides a boundary into lots of equal area with a single straight cut. Paste the boundary coordinates, say how many pieces you want — or the area each piece must be — and choose the direction the cut runs: parallel to a boundary line, through a bearing of your own, or the shortest cut that still works. You get the cutting line, the two points where it meets the boundary, and the area and perimeter of every piece. Acres, hectares, square metres and feet all at once, in your browser, no login.

Either projected coordinates — x, y or easting, northing in metres or feet, one vertex per line — or latitude, longitude in decimal degrees, which is recognised automatically and converted onto a local plane. Blank lines and lines starting with # are ignored. List the corners in order, clockwise or anticlockwise. Do not repeat the first vertex at the end.

How the calculation works

One straight cut, swept until the area matches

A single straight cut has one degree of freedom once its direction is fixed: how far along the normal it sits. So the split reduces to a one-dimensional search. Take the unit normal (a, b) of the chosen bearing, define the family of lines a·x + b·y = c, and compute the piece area to the positive side of the line as a function of c. That function is continuous and increases monotonically as the line sweeps across a convex polygon, so a bisection on c converges in a few dozen steps to the offset that leaves exactly the target area on one side.

Each candidate line cuts the polygon with the Sutherland–Hodgman algorithm: walk the boundary, keep the vertices on the wanted side, and where an edge crosses the line insert the intersection point. The crossings are not a by-product — they are the answer. They are the two coordinates a surveyor stakes to put the fence on the ground.

Area, and how the pieces are checked

The area of each piece comes from the shoelace formula, A = ½ |Σ (xᵢ·yᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ₊₁·yᵢ)|. Every piece is then summed and compared with the whole parcel: the difference is the area identity, and it is reported rather than hidden. A split that loses a square metre usually means the cutting line crossed the boundary more than twice — a concave parcel — and the same figure is what tells you so.

Several lots

With more than two pieces the cut is applied repeatedly: the first line is placed to leave the target area at the near side, the remaining polygon is cut again for the next lot, and so on. Each cut is therefore solved against the remaining piece rather than against the original parcel, which is what keeps the lots equal when the parcel is not a simple rectangle. The last piece is whatever is left, and it is reported even when it does not match the target.

Which bearing

Three options. Parallel to a boundary takes the direction of one of the parcel's own sides — the usual choice, because a line parallel to the frontage keeps every lot's frontage equal and the cuts run back from the road. A bearing you type allows a cut at, say, 090° to follow a fence, a contour or a drainage line. The shortest cut searches the bearings and returns the one whose cutting line is shortest, which is the least fencing for the same division.

No single straight cut can make every lot equal in area, frontage and depth at once — that needs three constraints and one line has only one degree of freedom. Decide first which of the three matters to the transaction, then choose the bearing that protects it.

Worked example

Take the default parcel — a 50 × 40 m rectangle, 2 000 m², or 0.4942 acres — and divide it into two equal lots with the cut running parallel to the 50 m front, which is side 1 of the default boundary.

The target is 2 000 ÷ 2 = 1 000 m² per lot. Every cut parallel to that front is a horizontal line at some distance d from it, and the strip behind it is a 50 × d rectangle. So 50 × d = 1 000 gives d = 20 m, the two lots are 50 × 20 = 1 000 m² each, and both keep the full 50 m frontage. The stake points are (0.000, 20.000) and (50.000, 20.000).

Three lots instead: each must be 2 000 ÷ 3 = 666.667 m², and a 50 × d strip reaches that at d = 13.333 m, so the cuts land at y = 13.333 and y = 26.667. Note what the third lot's figure does — it is not rounded separately but taken as the remainder, so the three areas add back to 2 000 exactly rather than to 1 999.999 or 2 000.001.

Now cut the same parcel at a bearing of 045° instead. The two lots still hold 1 000 m² each, but the cut is a diagonal and the frontages are no longer equal: the lot at the near corner takes a larger share of the front. That is the trade the bearing makes, and it is why a subdivision drawing normally works with cuts parallel to the road.

What to check before you stake it

  • The boundary closes. The tool reports the misclosure of the polygon after the cut — if the input had a typo, the area identity shows it up.
  • The cut stays inside the parcel. A bearing can be valid and still produce a line that clips a sliver at one end; read the two piece areas, not just the target.
  • Concave parcels. More than two crossings means one straight line does not divide the parcel cleanly. Split it into two convex parts first.
  • The units of the coordinates. Latitude and longitude are converted onto a local plane; projected coordinates are used as they are. Mixing the two in one entry is the commonest way to get a plausible but wrong parcel.
  • The governing rule. A planning scheme, a covenant or a by-law may set a minimum lot width or frontage. An equal-area split that produces an under-width lot is not a lawful subdivision, whatever the arithmetic says.

Frequently asked questions

How do I split a land parcel into equal areas?

Enter the boundary coordinates in order, set the number of pieces, and choose a bearing for the cutting line. The calculator finds the offset of a straight line parallel to that bearing which leaves exactly one n-th of the parcel on one side, cuts the polygon with it, and reports the area of each piece together with the coordinates of the two points where the line crosses the boundary. Once those points are staked, the line is on the ground.

Can I split a parcel into equal acres instead of equal pieces?

Yes. Set the split method to "Area per piece" and enter the acreage each lot must have. The calculator cuts one piece of that size at a time, working along the parcel from the chosen side, and stops when the remainder is too small for another full lot. The remainder is reported as a left-over piece with its own area, which is what you would actually convey.

Where does the cutting line start?

From whichever side you nominate. The drop-down offers the whole boundary as a choice of sides, and the cut is placed relative to that side — the first lot is the strip next to it. Changing the side is how you control which end of the parcel gets the first lot, and it usually matters because the frontage, the road access and the corner value are not the same at both ends.

Does the split keep the frontage equal?

Only when the cut runs parallel to the frontage. A line parallel to the front gives every lot the same frontage, because the front of the parcel is not touched — the cuts run back from it. A line at any other bearing gives equal areas but unequal frontages, which is often not what a subdivision wants even though the areas are right.

How accurate is the split?

The solver iterates the offset of the cutting line until the piece area matches the target to about one part in a billion of the parcel area, so the arithmetic is not the limit. The limit is the boundary: coordinates that were themselves rounded to the nearest metre cannot support a split to the nearest square metre. The calculator reports the closing error of the parcel and the area identity piece by piece so you can see whether the boundary adds up.

Why does the cut sometimes cross more than two sides?

A straight line through a convex parcel always meets the boundary in two places; through a concave one it can meet it in four or more, and then a single straight cut does not produce two clean pieces. The calculator reports the number of crossings, so a concave boundary is flagged rather than silently producing a shape that no fence can follow.

Can I use coordinates from a deed or a GIS in latitude and longitude?

Yes — the tool accepts latitude and longitude, and converts them onto a local plane centred on the parcel before splitting, so the cut is a straight line on the ground rather than a rhumb line on a map. If you already hold projected coordinates (State Plane, UTM, a local grid), enter those instead: the split is then done in exactly the coordinate system your survey uses, which is what a deed wants.

Is this land parcel split calculator free?

Yes, free, no login, no account, no usage limit. Every calculation runs locally in your browser, so no boundary or ownership data is uploaded anywhere.
