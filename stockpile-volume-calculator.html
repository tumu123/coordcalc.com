Stockpile Volume Calculator

This stockpile volume calculator turns three or four tape measurements into a volume and a tonnage. Pick the shape the pile actually is — a cone, a truncated cone with a flat top, or a ridge with square or tapered ends — enter the base, the height and the length, and it returns the volume in cubic metres, cubic yards or cubic feet, the mass at your bulk density, and the face angle so you can check the measurement against the angle of repose the material can actually stand. Stockpile volume —

How the calculation works

The three shapes, and one formula that covers two of them

A stockpile is one of three solids. A cone is what comes out of a conveyor or a belt stacker: circular base, single crest. A truncated cone is the same pile levelled off, so it has a flat circular top. A ridge is what a tipper leaves along a haul road: a long prism with a triangular or trapezoidal cross-section.

The cone and the truncated cone are the same formula with the top radius set to zero: V = πh ÷ 3 × (R² + R·r + r²), where R is the base radius, r the top radius and h the height. Put r = 0 and it collapses to V = πR²h ÷ 3, the cone. That is why the calculator asks for a top diameter rather than a separate shape: leave it at 0 and you have a cone.

Ridge piles: the body plus the ends

The body of a ridge is a prism of trapezoidal section, V_body = ½ × (W + w) × h × L, where W is the base width, w the top width (0 for a sharp crest) and L the length along the crest. The ends are the part most people forget. They are not vertical faces — the pile slopes back at the same angle as its sides, so each end is half a cone (or half a truncated cone), and the two halves together are exactly one whole one: V_ends = πh ÷ 3 × (R² + R·r + r²) with R = W ÷ 2 and r = w ÷ 2. The same frustum expression closes the formula for both shapes, which is why the two never disagree.

Tonnage from bulk density

mass = volume × bulk density. The density that belongs here is the bulk density as stockpiled, voids and all — a figure around 1.6 t/m³ for gravel, not the 2.6 t/m³ of the solid stone. The gap between the two is the whole point: a pile is mostly air. If the tonnage is going into a contract, weigh a container of known volume filled from the pile and use that number.

Face angle and angle of repose

The face angle of a pile is arctan(h ÷ (R − r)) for a round pile and arctan(h ÷ ((W − w) ÷ 2)) for a ridge. A loose granular material cannot stand steeper than its angle of repose, so the calculator works out what height the material could reach on the base you measured and compares. If your measured height needs a steeper face than the material allows, the pile cannot have those dimensions: the base is too small, or the height was taken from a different plane, or the material is damp and cohesive. It is the cheapest error check available on site and it costs nothing.

If the pile does not look like any of these — it has been partly loaded out, pushed into a corner, or has several peaks — three measurements will not describe it. Survey a grid of spot heights over the footprint and use the grid method instead, or fly it. A shape model is only as good as the resemblance between the pile and the shape.

Measuring a stockpile without a survey instrument

Most stockpile volumes are still taken with a tape, and most of the error comes from two habits: measuring the base at the wrong place and measuring the height from the wrong plane.

The base. The toe of a pile is a gradual transition, not a line. Walk the pile and find where the material is one particle deep, and measure there — measuring at the visible edge of the heap understates the base and therefore the volume. Take three widths about 60° apart and average them; a pile that has been loaded from one side is never a circle, and a single diameter will be wrong in a predictable direction.

The height. Measure from the same plane the base was measured on. If the base was taken on the yard surface and the height was taken with the tape hooked over the crest, the two numbers belong to different datums and the volume is out by the height of the hook. For a high pile, use a levelling instrument or a GNSS rover on the crest; a tape dropped down the face will follow the slope, not the vertical.

The shape. Look at the ends before you choose. A pile built by a tipper reversing along a line has tapered ends and the difference is not small: on a 20 m ridge 6 m wide and 2 m high the ends are 18.85 m³ of the 138.85 m³ total, about 14 percent. Getting that wrong is worth more than the precision of the tape.

Worked example

The default in the calculator is a cone with a base diameter of 10 m and a height of 3 m. The radius is R = 5 m, so V = π × 3 ÷ 3 × (25 + 0 + 0) = 25π ≈ 78.54 m³. At a bulk density of 1.6 t/m³ that is 78.54 × 1.6 ≈ 125.66 t, and the face angle is arctan(3 ÷ 5) ≈ 30.96°. Gravel at 37° could stand at 5 × tan 37° ≈ 3.77 m on that base, so the measured 3 m is flatter than repose and nothing is wrong — piles are normally built flatter than they could stand.

Change the top diameter to 4 m and it becomes a truncated cone. With r = 2 m, V = π × 3 ÷ 3 × (25 + 10 + 4) = 39π ≈ 122.52 m³ — 44 m³ less than the cone, because the top 2 m of the pile have been taken off. The face angle goes to arctan(3 ÷ 3) = 45°, which is steeper than gravel can stand, and the calculator says so: a flat-topped gravel pile 10 m wide with a 2 m top and 3 m sides is not something that forms by itself.

Now a ridge: base width 6 m, sharp crest, 20 m long, 2 m high. The body is ½ × 6 × 2 × 20 = 120 m³. With tapered ends, the two ends add one cone of radius 3 m and height 2 m, π × 2 ÷ 3 × 9 ≈ 18.85 m³, for a total of 138.85 m³. Untick the tapered ends and you get 120 m³ — a 14 percent difference on the same pile, decided by whether the ends were cut square or sloped back.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate the volume of a stockpile?

Measure the base and the height, then choose the shape that matches the pile. A cone is V = πR²h ÷ 3. A pile with a flat top is a truncated cone, V = πh ÷ 3 × (R² + Rr + r²). A long ridge is a prism, V = ½ × (W + w) × h × L, plus a cone or truncated cone for tapered ends. The default example here, a cone 10 m across and 3 m high, is 78.54 m³.

How do I measure a stockpile for volume?

Measure the base across the toe of the pile, not across the road, and take three widths at roughly 60 degrees to each other and average them, because a pile is never a perfect circle. Measure the height from the same base plane to the crest. For a ridge, take the length along the crest and the width at several points, and state whether the ends are tapered or square.

What angle of repose should I use?

Dry sand and gravel stand at about 34 to 40 degrees, crushed stone at 35 to 40, coal at 35 to 45 and grain at 25 to 30. The exact value depends on grading, moisture and how the pile was built. It matters here as a check rather than an input: if the measured height needs a face steeper than the material can stand, one of the measurements is wrong.

How do I convert stockpile volume to tonnes?

Multiply the volume by the bulk density of the material as stockpiled, not its solid density. Sand and gravel run about 1.5 to 1.7 t/m³, crushed stone 1.4 to 1.6, topsoil 1.0 to 1.3 and coal 0.8 to 0.9. Measure the density with a container of known volume if the tonnage is going into a contract.

Why do tapered ends add a third of a cone?

A ridge pile is a prism with a triangular cross-section, but its ends are not vertical faces; they slope back at the same angle as the sides. Each end is half a cone standing on the same base width, so the two ends together add exactly one cone of that radius and height, πR²h ÷ 3. On a 20 m ridge 6 m wide and 2 m high that is 18.85 m³ on top of the 120 m³ body.

Can I use this for a gravel or sand pile?

Yes. Pick the material preset to load a typical bulk density and angle of repose, then enter the dimensions you measured. The presets are midpoints of normal ranges, so replace them with your own test results whenever the tonnage is contractual.

What if the pile is irregular?

A pile that has been pushed, partly loaded out, or built against a wall is not a cone or a prism, and three measurements will only give an order of magnitude. Measure a grid of heights over the footprint and use the grid method on the cut and fill calculator instead, or survey it by drone or total station. The dimensioned shapes here are for piles that were built by a conveyor or a tipper and have not been disturbed.

How accurate is a volume from three measurements?

On a clean, freshly built cone, within about 5 percent, most of which comes from the base diameter because it enters the formula squared: a 2 percent error in the diameter is a 4 percent error in the volume. On a disturbed pile the figure is not a measurement at all, and it should not be used for inventory or payment.
