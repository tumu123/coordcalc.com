What an EPSG code actually identifies

An EPSG code is a short number that names one entry in the EPSG Geodetic Parameter Dataset — the registry of coordinate reference systems, datums, ellipsoids, units and transformations maintained by the IOGP. The registry grew out of the European Petroleum Survey Group, which is where the four letters come from, and it is now the common language that GIS software, GNSS receivers, web services and survey instruments use to say which coordinates they mean.

A code is written as authority:code, so EPSG:4326 is latitude and longitude on WGS 84 and EPSG:32617 is WGS 84 / UTM zone 17N. Because a code identifies a complete system rather than a place, the same field on the ground can honestly be described by several different codes at once: the position is the same, the numbers attached to it are not.

The important consequence is that a coordinate pair is meaningless until you know its code. The pair 630084.301, 4833438.586 is a perfectly good position in UTM zone 17N, and a position in the middle of the Atlantic if you read it as anything else.

The four kinds of code

Almost everything you will meet falls into one of four groups, and the group tells you what the numbers look like before you open the file.

KindWhat the coordinates areExamples
Geographic 2D Latitude and longitude, in degrees, on a stated datum. No projection involved. 4326 WGS 84 · 4269 NAD83 · 4277 OSGB36
Projected Easting and northing on a flat plane, in metres or feet, valid only inside the area the projection was designed for. 32617 UTM 17N · 27700 British National Grid · 2205 Kentucky North
Geographic 3D Latitude, longitude and ellipsoidal height — the raw output of a GNSS receiver. 4979 WGS 84 (3D)
Vertical A height only, measured from a named vertical datum rather than from the ellipsoid. 5703 NAVD88 height · 5773 EGM96 height

A compound code bolts a horizontal and a vertical system together — a State Plane zone with NAVD88 heights, for instance — which is what a set of survey observations usually is. The relationship between the ellipsoidal height in a 3D code and the elevation in a vertical code is the geoid, which is covered in geoid vs ellipsoid.

4326 or 3857? The pair that catches everyone

These two codes cover most of the world's data and they are constantly confused, because both carry the words WGS 84. They are not the same kind of thing at all. 4326 is geographic — degrees of latitude and longitude. 3857 is projected — metres of easting and northing, with the equator and the prime meridian at the origin.

There is a second trap inside 3857. A true Mercator projection carries the ellipsoid onto the plane, but Pseudo-Mercator replaces the ellipsoid with a sphere of radius 6,378,137 m. That makes the arithmetic simpler and the tiles square, and it also means the northings are systematically different from a genuine ellipsoidal Mercator such as 3395.

So the two are about 21 km apart in northing at 30° of latitude and 37 km apart at 60°. Neither is wrong — they answer different questions. But if a file says 3857 and was actually computed on the ellipsoid, or the other way round, a kilometre-scale disagreement appears that no amount of re-checking the survey will explain.

Pseudo-Mercator also inflates distance and area away from the equator: a scale factor of about 1.15 at 30°, 1.41 at 45° and 2.0 at 60°. Never measure area or distance on a Web Mercator layer. Use a projected zone such as a State Plane or UTM zone instead, or convert to latitude and longitude and work on the ellipsoid.

Codes that look alike but are not

Most coordinate mix-ups are not typos. They are two codes that differ by a plausible-looking amount.

TheseDiffer byWhy it matters
4326 vs 4269 About 1–2 m in North America WGS 84 and NAD83 are not the same datum. Fine for mapping, not for a boundary.
3857 vs 3395 21 km at 30°, 37 km at 60° Spherical versus ellipsoidal Mercator. Both are metres, so nothing looks obviously wrong.
32610 vs 26910 About 1–2 m The same UTM zone 10N maths on two different datums — WGS 84 and NAD83.
26910 vs 26929 Hundreds of kilometres The trap of the 26900 block: 26901–26923 are NAD83 UTM zones, 26929 upward are NAD83 State Plane zones. Same family, different tool.
4326 vs 4267 10–100 m in the United States NAD27 is a legacy datum. Anything older than the 1980s is probably on it.
26943 vs 2227 A factor of 3.28 — not 2 ppm Both are NAD83 / California zone 3. One is in metres, the other in US survey feet. Reading either as the other is the largest error on this page.
2227 vs 2222 2 parts per million, not 3.28× US survey feet and international feet are only 2 ppm apart, so a foot-based zone read in the wrong foot is a boundary-scale error, not a gross one. See survey foot vs international foot.

The unit is part of the code, and the blocks reflect that. On NAD83 the State Plane zones are split by unit: the 26900s and 32000s blocks are in metres, and the 2200s block holds the foot-based versions — 53 in US survey feet and 15 in international feet in the table above. A metre zone read as feet scales every coordinate by 3.28, which is obvious the moment you see it and invisible until you do. Two foot-based zones read as each other is subtler still: 2 parts per million looks like nothing until it reaches a boundary.

How to find the code for an area you have never worked in

  1. Ask what the coordinates are for. A national mapping agency will have a national grid; a state or province will often have its own zone; a project tied to a global data set will be in UTM. The purpose narrows the list faster than any search.
  2. Look for the datum in the metadata. WGS 84, NAD83, ETRS89, GDA2020, CGCS2000 and NAD27 each lead to a different family of codes, and the difference is usually stated somewhere in the file header, the service capabilities document or the drawing title block.
  3. Check the unit. Metres and feet split every family in two. If a northing is around 5,000,000 in metres it is a UTM-type coordinate; if it is around 16,000,000 it is probably feet, or a national grid with its own origin.
  4. Read the order of magnitude of the easting. About 500,000 means a UTM-type zone centred on a meridian. A six-figure easting in the 100,000s with a small northing means a local grid with its own false origin.
  5. Search the area name here. Type the state, country or zone name into the box above and the candidates appear with their units and areas of use, which is usually enough to identify the one that matches the extent of your data.
  6. Confirm with a known point. Convert one coordinate you can verify independently — a survey mark, a monument, a well-known landmark — and check that it lands where it should. One confirmed point is worth more than any amount of reading.

EPSG codes and Esri WKIDs

Esri software uses its own well-known identifiers alongside the EPSG ones. Many are identical — Esri also calls the British National Grid 27700 — but a substantial number are not, and the two registers do not always describe the same thing under the same number.

  • Web Mercator. Esri:102100 and its predecessor Esri:102113 both describe what EPSG calls 3857. A service quoting 102100 is quoting the same projection under a different authority.
  • North American State Plane. Esri assigns codes in the 102000s to State Plane zones whose EPSG equivalents sit in the 2200s and 26900s, so 102603 and 2205 can describe the same Kentucky zone in different registers.
  • Deprecated entries. Both registers retire codes. A code that still works in one package may already be flagged as deprecated in the registry, which is why it is worth checking a definition rather than trusting a number that has always been in the file.

When two systems disagree, the safest course is to send the full definition — the WKT or the PROJ string — rather than a number, because a number means nothing without its authority.

Frequently asked questions

What is an EPSG code?

An EPSG code is a short number that names one entry in the EPSG Geodetic Parameter Dataset — the registry of coordinate reference systems, datums, ellipsoids, units and transformations maintained by the IOGP. It is written authority:code, so EPSG:4326 is latitude and longitude on WGS 84 and EPSG:32617 is WGS 84 / UTM zone 17N.

What is the difference between EPSG 4326 and 3857?

4326 is geographic: degrees of latitude and longitude on WGS 84, the format used by GPS, GeoJSON and KML. 3857 is projected: metres of easting and northing, used by web map tiles. They are not interchangeable, and 3857 is a spherical Mercator, so its northings differ from a true ellipsoidal Mercator by about 21 km at 30° latitude and about 37 km at 60°.

Which EPSG code should I use for UTM?

On WGS 84, northern-hemisphere zones are 32601 to 32660 and southern-hemisphere zones 32701 to 32760, so UTM zone 17N is 32617 and zone 17S is 32717. On NAD83 in North America use 26901 to 26923, and on ETRS89 in Europe use 25828 to 25838.

How do I find the EPSG code for a State Plane zone?

Search the state name here rather than guessing the number. NAD83 State Plane zones are split by unit: the 26900s and 32000s blocks are in metres, while the 2200s block holds the foot-based versions, mostly in US survey feet. Kentucky is 2205 North and 26980 South in metres; California zone 3 is 26943 in metres and 2227 in US survey feet. Watch the neighbouring block too, because 26901 to 26923 are NAD83 UTM zones and not State Plane zones at all.

Why does the same area have several EPSG codes?

Because a code identifies a complete coordinate reference system, not a place. The same position can be described on WGS 84, NAD83 or NAD27, in metres or in US survey feet, in a local zone or in a UTM zone, and each combination is a separate registry entry with its own code.

Are EPSG codes the same as Esri WKIDs?

Often, but not always. Many Esri spatial references quote the same number as EPSG, and others use Esri-only identifiers: Esri:102100 and Esri:102113 both describe what EPSG calls 3857, and several State Plane zones carry Esri codes in the 102000s. Check which authority a file or service is quoting before treating the two as equal.

What does EPSG:32617 mean when written that way?

The text before the colon is the authority that maintains the definition and the number after it is the code within that authority. EPSG:32617, urn:ogc:def:crs:EPSG::32617 and the WKT or PROJ string exported by your GIS are three ways of naming the same system.

Where does this EPSG data come from?

The codes, names, units, areas of use and projection parameters are read from the EPSG Geodetic Parameter Dataset through the PROJ library, so nothing is typed in by hand and a deprecated entry is not presented as current. What you see is a reference subset chosen for surveying and mapping work, not the whole registry.
