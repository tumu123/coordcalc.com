Setting Out Calculator

This setting out calculator turns design coordinates into the two numbers a total station actually needs: the angle to turn from the backsight and the horizontal distance to the point. Enter the station and the backsight, paste the points to be set out, and you get a bearing, an angle and a distance for each one — with the circle reading you would see if the backsight is set to its own azimuth, and the turn order that keeps you working round the site in one direction. Angles can be read clockwise or anticlockwise, and adding the two heights gives the slope distance and the vertical angle as well. Metres or feet, all in your browser.

Easting first, then northing — the order a coordinate list is always written in. The backsight may be a target on a known point, or the reference object the instrument was zeroed on. If you have the backsight's own bearing instead of its coordinates, type the bearing into the field below and leave the point blank.

The four heights are optional and only affect the slope distance and the vertical angle. Leave them empty and the calculator works in plan, which is what the horizontal angle and the horizontal distance need. Fill the two levels and it also tells you what to set on the vertical circle.

How the calculation works

A bearing is just an angle from north

With eastings to the right and northings up, the direction from the station to a point is atan2(ΔE, ΔN), measured clockwise from north — which is exactly the whole-circle bearing a total station displays. The wrap to 0–360° is the only adjustment needed. Note the argument order: ΔE first. Swapping it is the single most common way to get a bearing that is right in two quadrants and wrong in the other two.

The horizontal distance is √(ΔE² + ΔN²). Both figures come from the same two subtractions, so they cannot disagree with each other, and they cannot disagree with the distance and bearing the reverse calculation would give.

Turning from the backsight

The instrument is oriented on the backsight, so what matters is not the point's bearing but the angle between the backsight direction and the point direction: angle = bearing(point) − bearing(backsight), taken modulo 360°. Turn clockwise and a positive angle is a right turn; read the instrument's left-angle scale and the same turn is 360° − angle. The calculator reports the side you select and labels the column, so the number always matches what the instrument will show.

The circle reading

Many surveyors do not turn angles at all: they set the horizontal circle to the backsight's bearing (or to zero) and then read the point directly. That is why the table has a circle reading column. If the circle is set to the backsight azimuth, the reading at the point is the point's own bearing — turn until the display shows the bearing in the table and the scope is on the point. The two methods give the same stake; the column just saves a subtraction in the field.

Slope distance and vertical angle

A total station measures a slope distance, not a horizontal one. If you fill in the station level and the design level of the point, together with the instrument and target heights, the vertical difference is ΔH = (level(point) − level(station)) + (instrument height − target height) and the slope distance is √(horizontal² + ΔH²). The vertical angle is atan2(ΔH, horizontal). Leaving the levels blank keeps the whole calculation in plan, which is what the horizontal angle and distance need anyway.

Before you turn the first angle

  • The station is on the control, not near it. A station set up 20 mm off the mark puts every point out by the same 20 mm, and no amount of care at the target recovers it.
  • The backsight is far enough away. An orientation error of 1″ moves a point by 5 mm at 1 km but only 0.5 mm at 100 m — and, more importantly, a short backsight makes the orientation itself uncertain. Prefer the longest reliable sight on the site.
  • Coordinate order. Easting first. A transposed pair still produces a plausible bearing and distance, which is why it survives review so often.
  • Which side the instrument reads. Right angles and left angles differ by the complement, not by the sign. Check the display before comparing it with the table.
  • A third point as a check. Measure to a point whose coordinates you already know and compare the distance with the calculated one. It costs a minute and catches the errors that the angles alone cannot show.

A worked example

Station at (1000.000, 2000.000), backsight on a target at (1000.000, 2100.000). The backsight is due north, so its bearing is 000.0000°.

A point at (1050.000, 2000.000) is 50 m due east: bearing 090.0000°, distance 50.000 m, and the angle to turn is 090.0000° clockwise. With the circle set to the backsight azimuth, the instrument reads 090.0000° — the bearing itself.

A second point at (1030.000, 2040.000) is 30 m east and 40 m north. Its bearing is atan2(30, 40) = 036.8699° and its distance is 50.000 m — the 3-4-5 triangle, which makes this a good example to check by hand. Turn 036.8699° clockwise from the backsight.

Now the same point with the instrument 1.500 m above the ground and the target at 2.000 m, the station level 100.000 m and the design level 100.500 m. The vertical difference is (100.500 − 100.000) + (1.500 − 2.000) = 0.000 m, so the slope distance equals the horizontal distance and the vertical angle is 0°. Change the design level to 101.000 m and the difference becomes 0.500 m: the slope distance grows to 50.0025 m and the vertical angle to 0.5730°.

Right angles, left angles and the circle

MethodWhat you do at the instrumentWhat the calculator givesWhere it is used

Set the circle to the azimuth Set the horizontal circle to the backsight bearing on the backsight The circle reading column — the point's own bearing Total stations, and any job with more than a few points
Left angles Read the anticlockwise scale The same column, computed on the other side Instruments and regions whose convention is left

The three methods produce the same stake on the ground. What differs is the number you read at the instrument, so the only real risk is comparing the wrong pair — a right angle from the table against a left reading on the display.

Frequently asked questions

How do I set out a point from a total station?

Set up over the station, sight the backsight, and either zero the horizontal circle on it and turn the angle the calculator gives, or set the circle to the backsight's bearing and turn until it reads the point's bearing. Then measure out the horizontal distance along that line — or the slope distance if the point is not at the same level — and mark it.

What is the difference between a bearing and an angle to turn?

A bearing is measured from north and belongs to the point. The angle to turn is measured from the backsight and belongs to the instrument's setup. The two are equal only when the backsight happens to sit due north; in general the angle is the point's bearing minus the backsight's bearing, modulo 360 degrees.

Should I use left angles or right angles?

Use whichever your instrument is set to display, and say which in the field book. The calculator computes on the side you select, so its column always matches the display. Right angles are the commoner convention, but instruments configured for left angles are still in daily use.

How do I get the slope distance instead of the horizontal distance?

Fill in the station level, the design level of the point, and the instrument and target heights. The vertical difference is the level difference plus the instrument height minus the target height, and the slope distance is the hypotenuse of that difference and the horizontal distance. Leave the levels blank and the calculator stays in plan.

Why does the calculator ask for the backsight as coordinates rather than a bearing?

Because the bearing comes from the two coordinates, and computing it removes a transcription step: the answer cannot disagree with the coordinates on the plan. If you already hold the backsight bearing, switch the backsight field to "a bearing I already know" and type it instead.

What is a circle reading?

It is what the horizontal circle displays at the point when the circle has been set to a reference direction. Set it to the backsight's bearing and the display at each point becomes that point's bearing, so you can turn until the reading matches and the scope is on the mark. Many total stations let you type the backsight azimuth in directly, which is faster than turning angles.

How accurate is a setting-out calculation?

The trigonometry is exact to the precision of the coordinates you enter. The limit is the control: coordinates rounded to the nearest millimetre give the bearing and distance to a millimetre, but a station set 20 mm off the mark moves every point 20 mm. Check the setup against a third known point before setting anything out.

Yes, free, no login, no account and no usage limit. Every calculation runs locally in your browser, so no coordinates are uploaded anywhere.
