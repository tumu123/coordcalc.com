How the calculation works

MGRS is a compact way of writing a UTM position. A reference such as 17T PJ 30084 33438 has four parts: the zone number (17), the latitude band letter (T), the 100 km square identifier (PJ) and the numeric digits that locate the point inside that square. The digits are always split evenly: the first half is the easting offset and the second half the northing offset, so the resolution depends on how many digits are printed — two pairs of five digits locate a point to 1 m, while four digits in total locate it to 1 km.

The latitude band is a letter from C to X covering eight degrees of latitude from 80°S, with the letters I and O left out to avoid confusion with 1 and 0. The 100 km square uses a different set of letters in each zone, and the row letters repeat every 2,000,000 m northwards, which is why the band letter must be present: without it a grid reference is ambiguous. This converter resolves that ambiguity by picking the candidate position that falls inside the stated latitude band.

Because MGRS sits on UTM, all values here use the WGS84 ellipsoid, and the standard zone numbering applies. Grid references near the poles are not defined: MGRS stops at 84°N and 80°S.

MGRS to Latitude and Longitude

Converting MGRS to latitude longitude reverses the forward routine. The converter splits the reference into its zone, latitude band, two-letter 100 km square and digits, rebuilds the easting and northing of that square, then projects the point onto the WGS84 ellipsoid. For example 17T PJ 30084 33438 decodes to latitude 43.642567 and longitude -79.387139, the ten-figure reference for the CN Tower in Toronto.

Because a 100 km grid square only fixes the point to the nearest 100 km, the latitude band does the heavy lifting: it is what lets the converter pick the correct repetition of the row letters and return a single, unambiguous position. A reference such as PJ 30084 33438 without its zone and band is therefore not enough to convert MGRS to lat long. The precision you get back is set by the digits you supply — five digits per axis resolves 1 m, three digits 100 m, and a bare 100 km square is the square itself.

If you need the underlying grid numbers rather than a geographic position, note that this page and the utm to lat long converter share the same WGS84 engine, so the easting and northing reported here match the UTM grid result to the millimetre.

When to use this tool

MGRS is the working reference format for military, emergency and search-and-rescue mapping, and it appears on plenty of civilian plans as well. Typical uses:

  • Reading a grid reference from a tactical map, incident log or radio message and plotting the position on a civilian map or GIS layer.
  • Writing a short, unambiguous position for a team in the field, where a ten-figure reference is easier to pass over the radio than a full latitude and longitude.
  • Recording a location from a GPS unit set to MGRS and checking it against a dataset held in latitude and longitude.
  • Preparing briefing material, exercise documentation or training scenarios that must quote grid references.
  • Cross-checking a hand-written grid reference against the coordinates recorded by a mapping application.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert an MGRS grid reference to latitude and longitude?

Enter the full reference including the zone number, the latitude band letter, the two-letter 100 km square and the digits, for example 17T PJ 30084 33438. The converter decodes the 100 km square, adds the digits, converts the easting and northing to geographic coordinates on WGS84, and returns latitude and longitude in both decimal degrees and DMS.

How accurate is an MGRS grid reference?

Accuracy depends on the number of digits. Two digits per axis (four digits in total) locate a point to 1 km, four digits per axis to 10 m, and five digits per axis to 1 m. The converter reports the applied resolution with every result.

Why are the letters I and O missing from MGRS?

Both letters are omitted so that they cannot be misread as the digits 1 and 0 on a map, a radio log or a handwritten field book. The latitude bands therefore run C, D, E, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, W, X, and the 100 km row letters skip I and O in the same way.

Why does the same MGRS reference appear in more than one place?

The 100 km row letters repeat every 2,000,000 m, so a reference without its latitude band letter could in principle match two or three positions north of the equator. The band letter removes that ambiguity, which is why a complete MGRS reference always includes it.

Does MGRS work anywhere in the world?

MGRS covers 80 degrees south to 84 degrees north, the same latitude range as UTM. Polar regions are covered by a different grid, the Universal Polar Stereographic system, which is not part of MGRS.
