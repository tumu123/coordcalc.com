Cut and Fill Calculator

This cut and fill calculator turns the sections you already measured into earthwork volumes. Enter cross sections station by station and it applies the average end area method; or enter a grid of depths over a pad, a lot or a borrow pit and it applies the corner-average grid method. Both modes report cut and fill separately, net the two, and apply a swell or shrinkage factor so the numbers match what the trucks and the compactor will actually do. Free, in your browser, no login.

Enter swell as a positive percentage (soil loosens on excavation, so 1 m³ in the ground becomes more in the truck) or shrinkage as a negative percentage (fill compacts to less than its bank volume). Leave at 0 for raw bank volumes.

Areas are in m² for metric units and ft² for imperial. Stations are distances along the alignment in the same length unit — station numbers such as 12+50 are accepted and read as 1250.

Spacing is the side of one square cell, in metres or feet to match the unit system. Each row of the grid below is one line; positive depths are cut and negative depths are fill.

Every row must have the same number of depths. With n rows and m columns the tool computes (n − 1) × (m − 1) cells, each by the average of its four corners.

Check the input — every line needs the same shape, and numbers must be readable.

How the calculation works

Average end area (cross sections)

Each pair of adjacent sections forms a prism. Its volume is the distance between the sections multiplied by the mean of the two areas: V = L × (A₁ + A₂) ÷ 2. Cut and fill are computed on the same pairs but kept apart, so a section that changes from cut to fill produces both a cut prism and a fill prism. A section with cut on one side of the centre line and fill on the other is normally split at the centre line before the areas are entered.

Grid method (corner average)

Over an area that is broad in both directions, depths are taken at grid nodes. Each cell of the grid contributes V = cell area × (d₁ + d₂ + d₃ + d₄) ÷ 4, where the four depths are the corners of that cell. Summing the cells gives the total, and because cut cells and fill cells are signed differently they can be summed separately. This is sometimes called the borrow pit method, and it is the natural companion to a survey of a pad or a stockpile.

Swell and shrinkage

The volumes above are bank volumes — the volume as measured in the ground. What leaves the site is a larger loose volume, because excavation breaks the soil apart and adds voids. What arrives in the fill is a smaller compacted volume, because compaction removes voids. The calculator applies your percentage to both cut and fill totals and reports the adjusted figures alongside the bank figures, so you can see the difference the factor makes.

Average end area or grid method — which to use?

MethodBest forField data neededTypical accuracy
Average end area Roads, railways, canals, pipelines, anything linear Cross-section areas at regular stations Within a few percent when sections are spaced at 15–30 m in changing ground
Grid (corner average) Building pads, parking lots, borrow pits, stockpiles, subdivisions Spot depths at the nodes of a regular grid Governed by the grid spacing; halving the spacing roughly quarters the error
Prismoidal High-value earthwork where a few percent is money Cross sections plus a mid-section area Closer than average end area, at the cost of extra survey work

The two methods are not competing answers to the same question, they are answers to different questions. A road is a long thin solid, so it is cut into prisms. A pad is a wide flat solid, so it is cut into cells. Where an alignment widens into a pad — an interchange, a lay-by, a treatment works — the earthwork is often split, with the linear part done by sections and the wide part by grid, and the two results added.

Swell, shrinkage and the three volumes you must keep apart

Almost every disagreement between a contractor and a designer about earthwork quantities is a disagreement about which volume is being counted. There are three, and they are different numbers.

VolumeWhere it existsUsed forTypical relation to bank volume
Bank (in situ)In the groundPayment quantities, project totals1.00 by definition
LooseIn the truck or the stockpileTruck counts, haulage, stockpile space1.05–1.45 depending on soil
CompactedPlaced and rolled in the fillBorrow quantity, fill capacity0.85–0.95 depending on soil and specification

The practical consequence is that cut and fill totals rarely balance even when the design intends them to. Suppose a site needs 10,000 m³ of compacted fill and offers 10,000 m³ of bank cut of the same material with a shrinkage of 10 percent. The usable fill from the cut is 9,000 m³ compacted, so roughly 1,100 m³ of bank borrow has to be imported — an outcome nobody would predict from comparing the two headline numbers. Run the conversion before the quantities are priced.

Worked example

Take the default sections in the calculator. Between station 0, with 12.4 m² of cut, and station 50, with 18.9 m² of cut, the average end area method gives 50 × (12.4 + 18.9) ÷ 2 = 782.5 m³ of cut. Between station 100 (7.2 m² cut, 3.1 m² fill) and station 150 (0 m² cut, 14.6 m² fill), the cut prism is 50 × (7.2 + 0) ÷ 2 = 180.0 m³ and the fill prism is 50 × (3.1 + 14.6) ÷ 2 = 442.5 m³. The cut prism ends where the cut area reaches zero, which is what makes the mid-section the natural transition point.

In grid mode, a 10 m grid with corner depths of 0.4, 0.6, 0.5 and 0.2 m gives an average depth of 0.425 m over a 100 m² cell, or 42.5 m³ of cut in that one cell. Four rows and four columns of depths create a 3 × 3 grid of cells, so the default grid above is nine cells and about 400 m² of ground — small enough to check by hand, which is exactly what a calculator of this kind should let you do.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate cut and fill volume?

For each pair of adjacent cross sections, multiply the distance between them by the mean of the two areas: V = L × (A₁ + A₂) ÷ 2. Compute cut and fill separately, then sum along the whole alignment. For wide areas, use the grid method instead and average the four corner depths of each cell.

What is the average end area method?

The standard earthwork method. It treats the ground between two sections as a prism whose volume is length times the mean end area. It suits anything linear — roads, rail, pipelines, canals — and is accurate to a few percent when sections are spaced at 15–30 m in ground that is changing.

When should I use the grid method?

When the area is wide in both directions rather than linear: a pad, a lot, a borrow pit or a stockpile. Depth is measured at grid nodes, each cell volume is its area times the average of its four corner depths, and the cells are summed.

What is the prismoidal correction?

A refinement of average end area. The prism assumption slightly overstates volume when two sections differ in shape as well as size. The prismoidal formula uses a mid-section area — V = L ÷ 6 × (A₁ + 4Am + A₂) — and worth applying on high-value earthwork.

Do I need to apply swell and shrinkage?

Yes, if the volumes are going to be used for truck counts, haulage, borrow or fill capacity. Bank volumes are what is in the ground; loose volumes are what you haul, and compacted volumes are what you place. Swell typically runs 5–45 percent and shrinkage about 5–15 percent, depending on soil.

How do I convert cubic yards to cubic metres?

Multiply cubic yards by 0.764554858 to get cubic metres, or multiply cubic metres by 1.30795062 to get cubic yards. Cubic yards come from cubic feet divided by 27.

How close should cut and fill balance?

On a balanced site, the compacted fill requirement should be compared with the usable compacted volume yielded by the cut, not with the bank volume of the cut. A cut of 10,000 m³ of bank soil at 10 percent shrinkage yields only about 9,000 m³ of compacted fill, so an apparently balanced site can still need imported borrow.
