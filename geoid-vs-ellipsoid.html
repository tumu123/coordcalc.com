Geoid vs Ellipsoid: Why Elevation Isn't Latitude

Latitude and longitude pin a point to a smooth mathematical surface, and that surface is not the one water finds. The ellipsoid is a tidy approximation of the Earth's shape; the geoid is the lumpy surface of equal gravitational potential that approximates mean sea level. They differ by tens of metres, and that difference is the reason an elevation from a GNSS receiver is not the elevation on a topographical map.

Two surfaces, two purposes

Defined byTwo numbers — semi-major axis and flatteningGravity measurements, satellite altimetry and a global gravity model
ShapePerfectly regularIrregular, undulating by up to about 100 m worldwide
Height measured from itEllipsoidal height, hOrthometric height, H — elevation above sea level

The ellipsoid exists because positioning needs a surface simple enough to do mathematics on. Every latitude and longitude you have ever seen is an angle measured from the ellipsoid, and the ellipsoidal height that comes with it is the distance above that surface along the normal. The geoid exists because gravity is what water, surveying instruments and drainage systems actually respond to, and the geoid is the surface that gravity defines.

The equation that links them

h = H + N

h = ellipsoidal height (from the ellipsoid, what GNSS gives)
H = orthometric height (above the geoid, what a levelling survey gives)
N = geoid height, or geoid undulation (the geoid's height above the ellipsoid)

Every practical problem in height comes from this one equation and from forgetting which term a particular number represents. Elevation on a map, on a bench mark, on a flood study — those are H, orthometric height, because they are derived from spirit levelling or from a geoid model. The position solution from a GNSS receiver gives h, ellipsoidal height, because that is what the geometry produces. They differ by N, which is tens of metres and which varies from place to place.

How big is the difference?

Worldwide, geoid undulations range over roughly 100 m, with the deepest low near the Indian Ocean. Over a single country the range is smaller but still far larger than any elevation precision anyone cares about in construction: across the continental United States the separation between the geoid and the ellipsoid is of the order of 10 to 35 m, changing gradually across the country.

That is why a receiver reporting an altitude of, say, 1,600 m is not necessarily telling you the site is at 1,600 m above sea level. It is telling you the height above the ellipsoid. Converting it needs N at that point, which comes from a geoid model — GEOID18 and its successors in the United States, EGM2008 or a national model elsewhere.

A test that catches the error immediately. Take a point with a published elevation and compare it with the raw ellipsoidal height from a GNSS receiver. A difference of tens of metres is normal and expected; a difference of a few metres is not, and a difference of nearly zero suggests the receiver has already applied a geoid model internally. That matters, because if the receiver applies one and your software applies another, you have subtracted the separation twice.

Datums, height and the difference from a horizontal datum

It is worth separating two questions that sound alike. A horizontal datum decides what latitude and longitude mean — which ellipsoid and which realisation of it. A vertical datum decides what zero elevation means, and is usually tied to a reference tide gauge plus a levelling network, with a geoid model used to extend it. In the United States, NAVD88 is the commonly used vertical datum, with a successor being rolled out from the mid-2020s onwards.

The two are independent. Changing the horizontal datum moves a point around on the map; changing the vertical datum changes the number attached to its height. A dataset can be correct in one and wrong in the other, and the error will only show up when it is merged with data that made the opposite choice.

What this means in practice

  1. Label heights. "Elevation 214.6 m" should say which vertical datum and, if it came from GNSS, whether a geoid model was applied.
  2. Do not mix levelled and GNSS heights silently. Spirit-levelled elevations are orthometric; raw GNSS heights are ellipsoidal. Averaging them without conversion produces a nonsense surface that still looks smooth.
  3. Use the right geoid model for the region and the epoch. A global model will get you within a metre; a modern national model will do much better.
  4. Remember that slope is unaffected. A constant offset in height does not change a gradient. If every point moves by the same amount, the slope between them is unchanged — which is why the error can persist unnoticed through years of grading work.
